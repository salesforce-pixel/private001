✓ The blood test on 26 August is the best in months — the clotting has corrected, the blood counts have fully recovered, and she is fit for the next chemotherapy cycle

Everything that was blocking a decision has moved the right way. Blood taken this morning — twenty days after the 6 August chemotherapy — shows the slow clotting has corrected, the blood counts have not just recovered but gone past where they started, and the liver enzyme that had been creeping up for three tests has turned around. She remains well and has now been fever-free for six weeks. Two things moved the wrong way, and they are the same signal; one result is genuinely puzzling and the PET-CT report is now overdue.

🟢 The clotting problem has largely resolved: the INR has come down from 1.74 to 1.21. ⚠ It is not quite back to normal — her own usual figure is 1.10 — but it is no longer the number that was putting every procedure on hold. Importantly, no vitamin K was given. That matters in two ways. It is further reassurance about the liver: an organ that had genuinely stopped making clotting factors does not recover them on its own in thirteen days. But it also means the cause was never actually tested. The best explanation remains that bile draining out through the external tube carries away the vitamin K the blood needs to clot — a good explanation, but still an explanation rather than a proven fact. This is now the third time the clotting has risen and settled by itself (June, July, and now August), which makes a fourth time a question of when rather than whether — and it will most likely happen around a procedure. The sensible ask is to request vitamin K in advance of any drain or port procedure, with a clotting test on the day, rather than waiting for it to rise again.

The blood counts have fully recovered. Platelets have gone from 135 back up to 300 — higher than before the chemotherapy — the haemoglobin has risen from 10.2 to 11.1 with no transfusion and no iron, and the infection-fighting white cells are comfortably normal. The dip that follows every gemcitabine cycle has been and gone. Kidney function is also normal. ⚠ Her lowest point was expected around day 10–14 and was never measured, which was a real gap at the time — but a dip deep enough to matter would not have bounced back above its starting point by day 20. Taken together, the blood results no longer stand in the way of the next chemotherapy cycle. The one thing still needed to plan it is the PET-CT report.

The liver enzyme that was climbing has turned around. ALT has fallen from 49 to 37 and AST from 60 to 48, breaking a rise across three consecutive tests. This matters for a specific reason: the leading suspicion had been an immune reaction to durvalumab, the third dose of which was given on 6 August. That kind of reaction builds over weeks and goes up, not down — so a level that peaked at day 7 and is falling by day 20 is the wrong shape for it. Combined with hepatitis B already being undetectable, the more likely explanation is simply the residual bile-duct blockage. That is a meaningful piece of reassurance about the immunotherapy.

⚠ The genuinely puzzling result — and the reason to read the PET carefully: the bile-duct markers have split apart for the first time. To be careful about this: it is better read as ALP failing to improve alongside everything else than as ALP rising, because this figure bounces around a lot (465, then 667, then 483, now 536). Normally these two markers move together, so their separation says something. The 22 August scan showed the drained parts of the liver are decompressed — so if a bile-duct marker is still stuck high, it is most likely coming from a part of the liver the drains do not reach: the undrained right-posterior branch that two scans in a row have not commented on.
