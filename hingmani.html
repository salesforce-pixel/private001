🔑 What “unconfirmed” actually means, because it is not a formality: she is on durvalumab, an immunotherapy drug. Immunotherapy can make a tumour look bigger and more active on a scan when what is actually happening is that immune cells have flooded into it — this is a well-recognised effect with a name, pseudoprogression, and a single scan cannot tell it apart from real growth. Because of this, cancer specialists use a separate set of rules for immunotherapy scans, and those rules deliberately do not allow progression to be declared on one scan. The finding is labelled “unconfirmed”, and the required next step is a second scan, usually four to eight weeks later, to confirm or overturn it. Those same rules expressly allow treatment to continue in the meantime if the patient is well — and she is: no fever, blood counts fully recovered, bilirubin normal for the first time in this illness, liver enzymes improving, haemoglobin rising without transfusion, and she walked out of a drain replacement the same day, six days ago, and her mild post-procedure temperature settled without any antibiotic. This is not an argument that the report is wrong. It is an argument that it is not final, and that what happens next is a considered decision by her oncologist rather than an emergency.

The new lymph nodes are the finding that drives the conclusion — under these rules, disease appearing somewhere it was not before counts as progression regardless of how much anything has grown, and it is much harder to explain away than a measurement that has moved a little.
