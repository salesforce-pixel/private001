Snapshot as of 5 August 2026 · At home · fever-free 21 days · chemotherapy due to restart 6 Aug

⚠ Well at home and cleared for chemotherapy tomorrow — but two new blood results need raising before the infusion

She is well — fever-free for 21 days and off all antibiotics for a fortnight. Blood tests taken yesterday evening confirm she is fit for the chemotherapy dose planned for tomorrow, 6 August: blood counts, kidney function, clotting and bilirubin all pass. Two results on the same panel, however, need to be put to the treating team before the drip is started.

The first is her sodium. It has fallen from 135.2 to 125.7 in ten days — a level lower than when she was admitted to the emergency room with a bile-duct infection on 8 July. The laboratory flagged it and re-ran the sample to be sure, so it is a real result. What makes it worth asking about is that the usual explanation no longer fits: every previous dip was attributed to cisplatin, and she has not received cisplatin for 30 days. The likeliest causes now are salt lost through the external bile drain (bile is salty, and her left drain has been draining outside the body since 8 July), low salt intake, an under-active thyroid, or — less likely, but the one worth excluding before another immunotherapy dose — a hormone-gland side effect of durvalumab. This matters most for the cisplatin part of tomorrow's treatment, which itself causes salt loss. One practical warning: hyponatraemia is often treated by restricting fluids, and that would be the wrong move if the cause is salt loss — the simple test that distinguishes the two is a paired blood and urine osmolality with a urine sodium.

The second is a bile-duct enzyme called ALP. It has risen from 465 to 667 — the highest level in all 42 blood tests on record, higher even than during the acute infection in July — while her bilirubin stayed normal and is still falling. That specific combination usually means a part of the liver is blocked but cannot show up in the bilirubin because its bile has nowhere to drain. This is exactly the result the family tracker predicted in advance on 26 July as the thing that would re-open the question of her undrained right-posterior bile segment. The two innocent explanations offered then — a different laboratory, and a lingering antibiotic effect — no longer apply. The two real possibilities are a segment that is progressively blocking or the cancer progressing after 30 days without treatment. Neither can be settled by another blood test: it needs a scan and the CA 19-9 tumour marker. See Labs and Care plan.

To be clear about what this does not mean: neither finding is a reason to refuse tomorrow's treatment. Thirty days without chemotherapy against an untreated cancer is the bigger risk, and if the enzyme rise reflects the cancer progressing, that is an argument for treating, not against. The purpose of raising both is that the team sees them and decides knowingly — particularly about the cisplatin component — rather than finding them afterwards.

Also on this panel, and genuinely good: haemoglobin is still climbing (11.3, no transfusion since 11 July), platelets 206, albumin back in the normal range for a second time, and the previously-low kidney markers have all normalised — the nutrition plan is visibly working. A red-cell measure called RDW fell for the first time, which supports the view that her anaemia has been recovering marrow rather than a vitamin deficiency. Not so good: the tumour marker CA 19-9 and the hepatitis-B viral load were missed for the seventh time running — on a panel the family booked themselves, so no doctor had to be asked.
