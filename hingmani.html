✓ Chemotherapy restarted and the tumour marker has fallen 47% — but a new clotting result needs raising today

The treatment went ahead. Chemotherapy (Cycle 3 Day 8 — gemcitabine, cisplatin and the third dose of durvalumab) was given on 6 August after a 31-day gap, and she came through the week well and fever-free. Blood tests taken this morning are the first full panel in eight weeks — and they answer most of the questions that were open. Three of the four worries raised on 5 August have resolved. One new problem has appeared, and it is the correctable kind.

The best result first: the tumour marker has fallen sharply. CA 19-9 — missed on seven consecutive blood panels — was finally measured and has dropped from 469 to 248, a 47% fall, and 63% below its peak of 677 in May. That clears the response target the oncology team had set. It also answers the question the 5 August panel could not: the worry then was that the rising bile-duct enzyme might mean the cancer progressing during the treatment gap. This says it is not — and it says so with something to spare, because a blocked bile duct pushes this marker up, and hers was blocked throughout. The related marker CEA is normal.

Second, the bile-duct enzyme has come back down. ALP has fallen from 667 to 483 and GGT from 509 to 398, both at the same laboratory — so the alarming 5 August figure was a spike rather than a steady climb, and this page's reading then was one test too early. It is worth being straightforward about that. ⚠ It has not gone away, though: 483 is still about four times the normal upper limit, and the bilirubin has ticked up slightly. A scan is still the right next step — and one is already due (see below).

Third, the sodium is correcting on its own — 125.7 up to 130.4 in eight days, at a safe rate, with chloride following. Still below normal, but no longer alarming. Two of the possible causes can now also be crossed off: the thyroid test came back with a normal free T4, which rules out an underactive thyroid as the cause and, more usefully, makes the hormone-gland side effect of durvalumab unlikely — that condition produces the opposite thyroid pattern to hers. And the long-running anaemia question has closed: vitamin B12 is normal, and the iron studies — repeated cleanly a month after the iron injection, exactly as planned — confirm this is anaemia of long-term illness, not iron deficiency. So iron should stay stopped.

🔴 The new problem, and today's job: her blood is clotting more slowly than at any point in this illness. The INR has risen from 1.10 to 1.74 in eight days — higher than the previous peak of 1.53 in June — and her platelets have dropped to 135. What makes this reassuring rather than frightening is the albumin, which is normal at 3.60. If the liver itself were failing, that protein would fall alongside the clotting factors — it has not. The likely explanation is much simpler: bile has been draining out of her body through the external drain for 36 days, and bile is needed to absorb vitamin K, which the blood needs to clot. That means the treatment and the test are the same thing — give vitamin K, repeat the clotting test, and the answer confirms itself within a day. Ask for it today. The bleeding risk here is the combination of the slow clotting and the low platelets, not either one alone.

About the falling blood counts: platelets, haemoglobin, white cells and neutrophils have all dropped together — and that is expected. This is day 7 after chemotherapy, and this is what gemcitabine does to the bone marrow every cycle. Encouragingly, it is milder this time than in either of the two previous cycles at the same point (platelets 135 now, against 50 and 96 before). It will dip further before it turns, so a repeat blood count around 16–20 August is worth booking — together with a repeat clotting test.

Two things still open. The hepatitis-B viral load was finally ordered but the result has not come back yet — worth chasing, because her ALT liver enzyme has now risen on three tests in a row. And her inflammation markers remain clearly raised (CRP 35.9, ESR 109) despite 29 days without fever, a normal white count and a clean urine — which is what long-term bacteria living in the bile drains cost the body, and the strongest argument for the stenting discussion. One date to put in the diary: the oncologist planned an interim PET-CT scan for 10–12 days after this chemotherapy dose — that is 16–18 August, now — and it is the same scan that answers the bile-duct question. One appointment, two answers.
